# Quarter 13 review: three labor markets, one quarter

**For Vikram, before merging PR #17.** Quarter 13 (Q1 2030) is the factor markets from the Week 13 package: the Norwegian union, the Permian wage, and Baton Rouge's turnaround at the contractor peak. This note has everything a student reads, what each advisor knows, the economics as built, and the calls to confirm. Local play-through done with three demo teams answering differently.

## 1. The quarter in one paragraph

Three labor markets, built three ways. In Norway one union faces one employer and the government can end a strike by arbitration; the union asks for 8% on a $420M wage bill, and Norway's 78% tax means a concession costs Halden 22% of its face value. In the Permian Halden takes the market wage ($145k) because a marginal worker earns it $507.6k a year; the question is how to keep people, not what to pay. On the Gulf Coast contractor crews cost 35% more at the spring peak, and Baton Rouge's turnaround is due: $81M now, or an expected $78M off-peak next quarter (60 plus a 12% chance of a $150M breakdown), for three points of plant condition. Two one-time answers; the Permian is analysis.

## 3. What's happening (briefing)

**Q1 2030 · Monday 7 January · Houston · Three labor markets, one quarter**

The Norwegian offshore union opened the year with a letter: 8% on the wage bill, or the platforms stop. The wage bill is $420 million a year, so that's $33.6 million. Bjørn says the union means it. Kwame says the Norwegian government has ended every offshore strike of the last twenty years by ordering arbitration, and the arbitrators have never awarded less than the union's industry rate. Ana says to remember who pays Norway's bills before anyone gets angry.

In Texas it's the opposite problem. Nobody is striking; people are leaving. The Permian market pays about $145,000 a year loaded and Halden has to pay it or lose crews to the company next door. The question there isn't what to pay. It's what a worker is worth.

And at Baton Rouge, the turnaround is due. Every refinery on the Gulf Coast wants contractor crews in March, and the crews know it: the rate is 35% above what it costs in the summer. You can do the work now at the peak, or run the plant past due and do it off-peak next quarter, with what that means for a plant that's overdue for maintenance.

**The big question:** What does the same word, labor, mean in three different markets, and how should Halden answer each one?

**How it works (rules box):** Two one-time decisions this quarter. The Norwegian union, on the Oil fields page: accept the 8% and it lands on the $420 million wage bill for good, $8.4 million a quarter; Norway's 78% petroleum tax then absorbs most of it, so the cost to Halden after tax is about 22% of the face value. Offer less, or refuse, and the union stops the operated fields; the stoppage costs the margin on those barrels for as long as it lasts, and the government can end it by arbitration. Baton Rouge's turnaround, on the Refineries page: do it now and the contractor crews cost $81 million at the peak, under Refineries this quarter. Wait, and it costs $60 million next quarter off-peak, with a 12% chance that the plant breaks down first, which costs $150 million, and three points off plant condition now for running past due. Whether it breaks down is decided for your class at next quarter's close. The Permian is analysis, not a decision: the exhibit shows what a marginal worker earns Halden against what the market pays.

The rules box tells students a stoppage happens and that the government can end it, but not how long each stoppage lasts; Osei's facts and Priya's predictions carry that.

**Marchetti's line on the Prices page:** Oil is $71 and carbon crept to $45; neither is the story. The story is three labor markets that don't behave alike. In Norway one union faces one employer, and the price is whatever the two of them, and the government, settle on. In Texas Halden takes the market's price. On the Gulf Coast the price moves with the season. Work out which market you're in before you decide what to pay.

## 7. Industry news

**Oslo keeps the peace.** The Norwegian offshore unions opened the year with claims of 7% to 9% across the shelf. Two operators settled at once. One held out, lost two weeks of production to a strike, and received the same award from the government's arbitrators that the others had agreed to freely.

**The Permian can't find people.** Loaded wages in the Permian passed $145,000 this winter, and crews still move for a few thousand more. Operators that compete on wage lose; the ones holding crews are doing it with schedules, housing and the promise of work next year.

**March on the Gulf Coast.** Eleven refineries want turnaround crews in the same six weeks. Contractor rates are 35% above summer, and the companies that waited for cheaper crews last year are the ones with the unplanned outages this year, or so the companies that paid the peak like to point out.

## 10. Calls to confirm

1. **Stoppage lengths and the arbitration outcome are my design** (constants `strike_weeks_refuse` 2, `strike_weeks_half` 1, settled at 8% by arbitration after a refusal). The package has the tax shield and the benchmarks but says nothing about what a strike does. Norway's real practice (compulsory arbitration ending offshore strikes) is what the content leans on. If you'd rather a refusal held the wage bill at 0% with a longer stoppage, that's a constant and a story edit.
2. **The turnaround draw is per class at the Q14 close**, 12%, stored on the quarter like the OPEC outcome; the instructor can preset `outage` or `no_outage`. Every team that waited shares the same draw. The expected-value version ($78M booked regardless) would have been the KPI package's method, but a plant either breaks down or doesn't.
3. **The Norwegian tax shield is modelled on the raise only.** The model's tax line uses a flat rate on all of Halden; Norway's 78% is applied to the wage raise so the 22% lesson shows on screen ("Norway's tax gives $6.6M of this back"). The stoppage's margin is not shielded in the tax line (the story says most of it "would have been tax anyway" in words, which is true in Norway but not in the model's flat tax). I'd rather keep the model simple than add a Norwegian tax regime two quarters from the end; say so if you disagree.
4. **The Permian has no decision.** The package's Permian content is a valuation (MRP against the market wage); the lesson is that Halden is a wage-taker. The exhibit and advisors carry it; nothing is set on a page.
5. **Turnaround on the Refineries page, not Big projects.** The design table put "contractor timing" under Capital; it's Baton Rouge's maintenance, and students will look for it under Refineries.
